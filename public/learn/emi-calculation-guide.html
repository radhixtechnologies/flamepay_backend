How EMI is calculated — and how to lower it

The EMI formula explained in plain language, worked examples for personal and home loans, and practical ways to pay less interest.

8 min read · Updated 18 Nov 2026
In this guide

What an EMI is

An EMI — equated monthly instalment — is the fixed amount you pay your lender every month until a loan is repaid. Each EMI has two parts: interest on the amount you still owe, and principal, which reduces the loan itself. In the early months most of your EMI goes towards interest; towards the end, most of it goes towards principal. The total stays the same each month (for a fixed rate), but the split keeps changing.

Understanding how the EMI is worked out helps you compare loans properly, choose a tenure you can afford, and spot ways to save a lot of money in interest.

The EMI formula

Lenders use a standard formula for reducing-balance loans:

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n is the number of monthly instalments.

You never need to work this out by hand — the FlamePay EMI calculator does it instantly — but knowing what goes into it shows you which levers matter: the amount, the rate and the tenure.

Worked example 1: a personal loan

Say you borrow ₹5,00,000 as a personal loan at 11% a year for 3 years.

  • Monthly rate r = 11 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.009167
  • Number of instalments n = 3 × 12 = 36
  • EMI ≈ ₹16,369

Over 36 months you pay about ₹5,89,300 in total — roughly ₹89,300 of that is interest.

Worked example 2: a home loan

Now a home loan of ₹40,00,000 at 8.5% a year for 20 years.

  • Monthly rate r = 8.5 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.007083
  • Number of instalments n = 20 × 12 = 240
  • EMI ≈ ₹34,700

Over 20 years you pay about ₹83 lakh in total — more than ₹43 lakh of it is interest, which is more than the loan itself. That's normal for long home loans, and it's exactly why small changes in rate and tenure matter so much.

How tenure changes the picture

A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises the total interest you pay. Here is the same ₹40 lakh home loan at 8.5% over different tenures (figures rounded):

TenureMonthly EMITotal interest paid
10 years≈ ₹49,600≈ ₹19.5 lakh
15 years≈ ₹39,400≈ ₹30.9 lakh
20 years≈ ₹34,700≈ ₹43.3 lakh
25 years≈ ₹32,200≈ ₹56.6 lakh

Going from 20 to 25 years saves only about ₹2,500 a month in EMI but costs over ₹13 lakh more in interest. The best tenure is usually the shortest one whose EMI you can pay comfortably every month, with room for emergencies.

How the interest rate changes the picture

For the same ₹40 lakh over 20 years, even half a percent makes a real difference:

Interest rateMonthly EMITotal interest paid
8.0%≈ ₹33,500≈ ₹40.3 lakh
8.5%≈ ₹34,700≈ ₹43.3 lakh
9.0%≈ ₹36,000≈ ₹46.4 lakh

A 0.5% lower rate saves roughly ₹3 lakh over the life of this loan. That's why your credit score, which affects the rate you're offered, is worth improving before you apply.

Fixed vs floating rates

With a fixed rate, your EMI stays the same for the fixed period. With a floating rate, the rate moves with the lender's benchmark, so your EMI or tenure can change over time. Most home loans in India are floating-rate.

When a floating rate goes up, lenders usually extend your tenure first and keep the EMI the same. RBI rules require lenders to tell you about such changes and give you options — for example, to increase the EMI instead of the tenure, or to switch to a fixed rate where offered. Keep an eye on your loan statements so a rising rate doesn't silently add years to your loan.

7 ways to lower your EMI or total interest

1. Improve your credit score before applying

A score of 750+ often gets you a better rate. Even a small rate cut adds up over a long loan. See our guide on how to improve your CIBIL score.

2. Make a bigger down payment

Every rupee you don't borrow is a rupee you don't pay interest on. For home and car loans, a larger down payment also improves your approval chances.

3. Choose the shortest tenure you can comfortably afford

Use the calculator to find the EMI you can handle, then pick the shortest tenure that fits. A rough guide many people use: keep total EMIs under about 40% of your monthly take-home income.

4. Prepay when you can

Part-prepayments — for example, from a bonus — reduce your outstanding principal directly, which cuts future interest. Ask your lender to reduce the tenure rather than the EMI after a prepayment if you want to save the most. For individual borrowers on floating-rate loans taken for non-business purposes, RBI rules don't allow lenders to charge prepayment or foreclosure penalties; for fixed-rate and business loans, check your loan agreement.

5. Increase your EMI as your income grows

Raising your EMI by even 5–10% every year, when your salary goes up, can close a 20-year home loan years earlier.

6. Consider a balance transfer

If another lender offers a meaningfully lower rate, you can move your loan. Compare the savings against processing fees and other charges before switching.

7. Compare the total cost, not just the rate

Check processing fees, insurance bundled with the loan and other charges. For digital loans, the lender must give you a Key Fact Statement that shows the annual percentage rate (APR) — the all-in cost — before you sign. Use the APR to compare offers fairly.

Before you apply: a quick checklist

  • Your EMI fits comfortably within your monthly budget, with savings left for emergencies.
  • You've compared the APR and all fees from at least two or three lenders.
  • You know whether the rate is fixed or floating, and what happens when it changes.
  • You've read the prepayment and foreclosure terms.
  • You are dealing with an RBI-regulated bank or NBFC — and nobody has asked you for an upfront fee to "approve" the loan.

Flat rate vs reducing balance: a costly trap

Some lenders — often for consumer durables, two-wheelers or informal loans — quote a flat interest rate. A flat rate charges interest on the original loan amount for the whole tenure, even though you're repaying it every month. A reducing-balance rate charges interest only on what you still owe. Flat rates look much cheaper than they are.

Same ₹5 lakh, 3 yearsFlat 10%Reducing 11%
Total interest₹1,50,000≈ ₹89,300
Monthly EMI≈ ₹18,056≈ ₹16,369
Equivalent reducing-balance rate≈ 18%11%

A "10% flat" loan here actually costs about as much as an 18% reducing-balance loan. Always ask for the reducing-balance rate or the APR, and compare offers on that basis.

How much EMI can you afford?

Lenders look at your FOIR — fixed obligations to income ratio — the share of your monthly income that already goes to EMIs. Many lenders prefer total EMIs, including the new loan, to stay below roughly 40–50% of net monthly income. For your own comfort, aim lower: if your take-home pay is ₹60,000, keeping total EMIs around ₹20,000–24,000 leaves room for rent, savings and emergencies.

Before you commit, try a simple test: for three months, set aside the planned EMI amount into savings. If that feels easy, the loan is likely affordable. If it doesn't, consider a smaller loan or a longer tenure.

No-cost EMI: is it really free?

"No-cost EMI" offers on phones and appliances usually work by giving you an upfront discount equal to the interest, so the total you pay matches the product price. Two things are often not covered: a processing fee charged by the lender or card issuer, and GST on the interest component. Before choosing one, add up every charge and compare it with the straight purchase price — and remember the EMI still counts towards your monthly obligations and your credit report.

Frequently asked questions

Is a lower EMI always better?

No. A lower EMI usually means a longer tenure and more total interest. Choose a balance between a comfortable EMI and a reasonable total cost.

Does the EMI include insurance or fees?

Usually not. Processing fees are typically deducted upfront, and any insurance is a separate cost unless it is financed into the loan. Check your Key Fact Statement.

What happens if I miss an EMI?

You may pay a late fee and penal charges, and the missed payment is reported to credit bureaus, which lowers your score. If you're struggling, talk to your lender early.

Can I pay off my loan early?

Yes. Most loans allow part-prepayment or full foreclosure. Floating-rate loans for individuals (non-business) can't carry foreclosure penalties; for other loans, check the terms.
