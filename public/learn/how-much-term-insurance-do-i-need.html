How much term insurance do you really need?

A plain-language guide to choosing the right term insurance cover, policy term and riders — with simple rules of thumb and a step-by-step method.

7 min read · Updated 18 Nov 2026
In this guide

What term insurance does

Term insurance is the simplest and cheapest kind of life insurance. You pay a premium for a fixed period — the policy term — and if you pass away during that period, your nominee receives the sum assured. If you outlive the term, there is usually no payout. That is what keeps the premium low: you're paying only for protection, not for savings or investment.

Its job is simple: if your income stopped tomorrow, would your family be able to keep living the life they have now, repay loans and still reach their big goals? The cover you choose should make the answer "yes".

Two quick rules of thumb

Rule 1: a multiple of your income

A common starting point is to choose cover of 10 to 20 times your annual income. Younger people usually need a higher multiple, because their families depend on their income for more years.

Your ageTypical multiple of annual income
Under 3515–20×
35–4412–15×
45–548–10×
55 and above5–8×

So a 30-year-old earning ₹8 lakh a year might start at ₹1.2–1.6 crore of cover.

Rule 2: add your loans

Life cover should also clear your outstanding loans, so your family doesn't inherit EMIs. Add the outstanding amount of your home loan, car loan, education loan and any personal loans to the income-based figure.

A more precise method, step by step

Rules of thumb are a good start. If you want a more careful number, work through these five steps:

  • Step 1 — Yearly household expenses. How much does your family need every year to live comfortably? Don't include your own personal expenses that would stop.
  • Step 2 — Years of support needed. Until when would your family need this support — for example, until your youngest child finishes education and your spouse retires?
  • Step 3 — Outstanding loans. Add every loan your family would need to repay.
  • Step 4 — Big future goals. Add goals you'd want funded even without you: children's higher education, a child's wedding, a parent's care.
  • Step 5 — Subtract what you already have. Subtract existing life cover, savings and investments your family could use.

The result is the cover gap you should fill. It's normal for this number to be larger than you expect — that's the point of doing the exercise.

A worked example

Rahul is 32, earns ₹10 lakh a year and has a spouse and a five-year-old child.

The income-multiple rule (17× for his age) would have suggested about ₹1.7 crore too — the two methods often land close to each other.

Choosing the policy term

Choose a term that lasts until your family is financially independent of your income — usually until your planned retirement, around 60 to 65. A longer term costs a little more, but buying a second policy later, when you're older, costs a lot more. Don't stretch the term to 80 or 85 unless you have a specific reason; most people don't need cover after their dependants are settled and loans are repaid.

Riders worth considering

Riders are add-ons to your base policy. A few are worth a look:

  • Critical illness — pays a lump sum if you're diagnosed with a listed serious illness.
  • Accidental death benefit — pays an extra amount if death is due to an accident.
  • Waiver of premium — future premiums are waived if you become disabled or critically ill, and the cover continues.

Add riders only if they fill a real gap. A separate health insurance policy is still essential — term insurance does not pay hospital bills.

Things that matter when you buy

Disclose everything honestly

Tell the insurer the truth about your health, habits (including smoking and alcohol), job and existing policies. Non-disclosure is one of the main reasons claims get rejected.

Check the insurer's claim record

Look at the insurer's claim settlement ratio and how quickly it settles claims. IRDAI publishes these figures in its annual report every year.

Name your nominee — and tell them

Add a nominee and make sure they know the policy exists and where the documents are. Married men can also buy a policy under the Married Women's Property Act, which keeps the payout for the wife and children, protected from creditors.

Use the free-look period

After you receive the policy, you get a free-look period to review it. If it isn't what you expected, you can return it and get your premium back, minus allowed deductions. Check the exact period in your policy document.

When to review your cover

Review your cover every few years and at big life events: marriage, a new child, a new home loan, or a big jump in income. You can buy an additional term policy to fill the new gap rather than replacing the old one.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Relying only on employer cover. It usually ends when you leave the job, and it's rarely enough.
  • Buying too little to save on premium. Under-insurance defeats the purpose.
  • Mixing insurance and investment. Investment-linked plans often give much lower cover for the same premium.
  • Delaying the purchase. Premiums rise with age and with health conditions.

What decides your premium

Two people buying the same cover can pay very different premiums. The main factors are:

  • Age — the younger you are, the lower the premium. It's locked for the whole term when you buy.
  • Tobacco use — smokers typically pay significantly more.
  • Health — conditions such as diabetes or high blood pressure, and your BMI, can raise the premium.
  • Cover and term — a larger sum assured or a longer term costs more, though not proportionally.
  • Occupation and lifestyle — some high-risk jobs and activities raise premiums.

Insurers may ask for a medical test for larger covers. Take it seriously — a policy issued after proper underwriting is easier to claim on.

How a claim works

Knowing the claim process in advance makes things far easier for your family:

  • Inform the insurer as soon as possible, online or at a branch, with the policy number.
  • Submit documents — usually the claim form, death certificate, policy document, the nominee's ID and bank details, and medical or police records if relevant.
  • Insurer review — the insurer verifies the claim and may ask for more information. IRDAI rules set timelines for insurers to settle or decide claims.
  • Payout — the sum assured is paid to the nominee's bank account.

Keep a simple folder — physical or digital — with the policy, nominee details and insurer contacts, and tell your family where it is.

Term insurance for homemakers and the self-employed

A homemaker's work has real financial value — childcare, household management, caring for elders. Some insurers offer cover for homemakers, often linked to the spouse's cover. If you're self-employed, you have no employer cover at all, so your own term plan matters even more; income proof such as ITRs is usually needed for larger covers.

Lump sum or monthly income payout?

Many term plans let you choose how your nominee receives the money: a single lump sum, a fixed monthly income for a number of years, or a mix of both. A lump sum gives flexibility to repay loans at once. A monthly income can protect a family that isn't used to managing a large amount. A common middle path is a lump sum large enough to clear loans and fund big goals, with the rest paid as monthly income. Whichever you choose, discuss it with your family so they know what to expect.

Frequently asked questions

Is term insurance expensive?

It is the cheapest form of life insurance. For a young, healthy non-smoker, a large cover often costs much less than people expect. Premiums depend on age, health, habits, cover and term.

Do I get money back if I survive the term?

With a standard term plan, no. Some "return of premium" plans refund premiums at the end, but they cost significantly more.

Is the premium tax-deductible?

Under the old tax regime, premiums may be deductible under Section 80C within the overall limit. The new tax regime doesn't offer this deduction. Check with a tax advisor for your situation.
