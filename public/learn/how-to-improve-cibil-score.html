How to improve your CIBIL score — 12 steps that work

A practical, step-by-step guide to raising your CIBIL score — what moves the number, what doesn't, and how long it really takes.

9 min read · Updated 18 Nov 2026
In this guide

What a CIBIL score is, in one minute

Your CIBIL score is a three-digit number between 300 and 900 that sums up how you have handled credit — loans and credit cards — in the past. It is calculated by TransUnion CIBIL, one of the four credit bureaus licensed in India (the others are Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark). Banks and NBFCs look at this number, along with your full credit report, before they approve a loan or a credit card, decide your limit and set your interest rate.

A higher score tells a lender that you are likely to repay on time. That usually means faster approvals, higher limits and lower interest rates. A lower score does not lock you out of credit, but it often means smaller limits, higher rates or more paperwork.

Score rangeWhat lenders usually read into it
750 – 900Excellent. Best chance of approval and the lowest rates.
700 – 749Good. Approvals are likely; rates may be slightly higher.
650 – 699Fair. Some lenders approve, often with conditions.
300 – 649Needs work. Approvals are harder; focus on rebuilding.
NA / NHNo history yet. You need to start building credit.

What actually decides your score

Credit bureaus don't publish their exact formula, but the main factors are well known and consistent across bureaus. Roughly in order of importance:

  • Payment history — whether every EMI and card bill was paid on time. This is the single biggest factor.
  • Credit utilisation — how much of your total card limit you use. Lower is better.
  • Length of credit history — how long your accounts have been open. Older is better.
  • Credit mix — a healthy mix of secured loans (home, auto, gold) and unsecured credit (cards, personal loans).
  • New credit enquiries — how many times you applied for credit recently. Many applications in a short time are a warning sign.

Checking your own score is a soft enquiry and does not lower it. Only applications a lender makes on your behalf (hard enquiries) count.

12 steps to improve your CIBIL score

1. Pay every EMI and card bill on time — every single month

One missed payment can drop your score noticeably and stays on your report for years. Set up auto-debit for EMIs and at least the full card statement amount. If cash flow is tight one month, pay at least the minimum amount due before the due date to avoid a "late" mark — then clear the rest as soon as you can.

2. Pay the full credit card bill, not just the minimum

Paying only the minimum amount due avoids a late mark, but the unpaid balance attracts interest that is often 3–4% per month. The balance also keeps your utilisation high, which keeps your score down. Treat the minimum due as an emergency option, not a habit.

3. Keep card usage under 30% of your limit

If your total card limit is ₹1,00,000, try to keep your statement balance under ₹30,000. Using 80–90% of your limit every month signals that you depend on credit, even if you pay in full. Two easy fixes: pay part of the bill before the statement date, or ask your bank for a limit increase (without increasing your spending).

4. Don't close your oldest credit card

The age of your oldest account helps your score. If you have an old card with no annual fee, keep it open and use it for a small recurring payment — like a mobile bill — so the bank doesn't close it for inactivity.

5. Space out new loan and card applications

Every application leads to a hard enquiry. Several enquiries within a few weeks make you look credit-hungry. Before applying, check eligibility and compare offers, then apply only where you are likely to be approved. A gap of three to six months between applications is a sensible rule.

6. Check your credit report for errors

Mistakes happen — a loan you closed may still show as active, a payment may be marked late by error, or an account that isn't yours may appear. You are entitled to one free full credit report every year from each credit bureau. Read it line by line, not just the score.

7. Dispute errors with the bureau and the lender

If you find an error, raise a dispute on the bureau's website and inform the lender. Under RBI's rules, credit information companies and lenders must resolve complaints within 30 days, and you are entitled to compensation for delays beyond that. Keep screenshots and reference numbers.

8. Avoid "settling" a loan if you can

A "settled" status means the lender accepted less than the full amount. It hurts your score far more than a "closed" status and lenders see it for years. If you are struggling, talk to the lender early about restructuring or a revised EMI instead of settling.

9. Clear old dues and get a no-dues certificate

If an old card or loan has an outstanding balance, pay it off and ask the lender for a no-dues certificate. Then make sure the account shows as "closed" on your report within a couple of months.

10. Build a healthy mix over time

You don't need to take a loan just to improve your mix. But over the years, a combination of a card used responsibly and a secured loan repaid on time shows lenders you can handle different kinds of credit.

11. Starting from zero? Use a secured card

If you have no credit history ("NA" or "NH" score), a credit card against a fixed deposit is an easy way to start. Use it lightly, pay in full every month, and within six to twelve months you will have a score.

12. Be careful about co-signing and joint loans

When you co-sign or take a joint loan, the account appears on your report too. If the other person pays late, your score drops. Only co-sign when you are confident about repayment — or ready to pay yourself.

How long does it take to improve a score?

There is no instant fix — anyone promising to "boost" your score overnight for a fee is not genuine. Lenders now report data to bureaus every fifteen days, so positive changes like a paid-down card balance can show up within a month or two. Recovering from serious issues such as missed payments or a settled loan takes longer — usually twelve to twenty-four months of clean repayment.

ActionWhen you may see an effect
Paying down card balancesNext one or two reporting cycles
Correcting an error on your reportAfter the dispute is resolved (usually within 30 days)
Twelve months of on-time paymentsSteady improvement over the year
Recovering from a settlement or defaultTypically 12–24 months or more

Myths that don't help your score

  • "Checking my score lowers it." False. Your own checks are soft enquiries.
  • "Closing all my cards will raise my score." Usually false. It can raise utilisation and shorten your history.
  • "Income affects my CIBIL score." False. Income affects loan eligibility, but it is not part of the score.
  • "Debit card and UPI spending build my score." False. Only credit products — loans and credit cards — are reported.
  • "Paying someone can delete bad history." False. Accurate negative information cannot be removed; only errors can be corrected.

A simple 90-day plan

  • Week 1: Get your free credit report, list every account, and flag anything that looks wrong.
  • Week 2: Set up auto-debit for every EMI and card bill. Raise disputes for any errors.
  • Month 1–3: Bring card usage under 30% of your limits. Avoid new applications.
  • Month 3: Check your score again and compare. Keep the habits going — the score follows.

How to read your credit report, section by section

The score is a summary; the report is the detail lenders actually read. Most reports have four parts.

Personal and contact details

Your name, date of birth, PAN, addresses and phone numbers. Check these first — a wrong PAN or a mixed-up name is a common reason someone else's account ends up on your report.

Accounts

Every loan and credit card you have, open or closed, with the lender, type, sanctioned amount or limit, current balance and status. Make sure closed accounts show as "closed", not "active" or "settled".

Payment history (DPD)

Each account shows a month-by-month grid of DPD — days past due. "000" or "STD" means paid on time. Numbers like "030" or "090" mean the payment was that many days late. Even a single "030" pulls the score down, so this is where to look first if your score dropped.

What you seeWhat it means
000 / STDPaid on time — standard account
001–089Paid late by that many days
SMASpecial mention account — early sign of stress
SUB / DBT / LSSSub-standard, doubtful or loss — serious default
XXXNo data reported for that month

Enquiries

A list of lenders who checked your report when you applied for credit. Many enquiries in a short period are a red flag. If you see an enquiry you don't recognise, it could mean someone applied in your name — raise it with the bureau.

If your loan or card application was rejected

A rejection isn't the end — it's information. Ask the lender for the reason; it is usually one of these: a low score, high existing EMIs compared with your income, too many recent enquiries, or a mismatch in your documents. Fix the specific reason, wait at least three months before applying again, and consider a secured product in the meantime to keep building your history.

Building credit as a student or first-time borrower

Everyone starts with no history. The fastest safe route is one small, well-managed product: a lifetime-free card or a card against a fixed deposit, used for a few regular bills and paid in full every month. Avoid "buy now, pay later" offers and small app loans you don't need — they are reported too, and a missed ₹2,000 instalment can hurt as much as a missed big EMI. After six to twelve months of clean history, you'll usually have a score in the 700s and better options open up.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good CIBIL score for a personal loan?

Most lenders are comfortable at 750 and above. Many lenders also approve applications in the 700s, sometimes at a slightly higher rate. Below 700, approval depends heavily on the lender and your income.

Can I get a loan with a low score?

Sometimes, yes — through secured loans like a gold loan or a loan against a fixed deposit, or with a co-applicant who has a strong score. Use these to rebuild your history with on-time payments.

Why is my score different on different apps?

Different apps may show scores from different bureaus, or data updated on different dates. Small differences are normal. Big differences are worth a closer look at each report.

How often should I check my score?

Once every month or two is plenty. Checking more often doesn't hurt your score, but the number usually only changes after lenders report new data.
